Argentina

In the 2023 elections, Javier Milei won a resounding victory against Economy Minister Sergio Massa. During the previous Peronist government, annual inflation had reached 138 per cent, and Milei’s promise to bring it under control found fertile ground among voters desperate for change. His win marked the beginning of a dramatic ideological shift in Argentina and the wider region; which had been dominated by left-wing governments for much of the preceding decade. Milei describes himself as an “anarcho-capitalist” who sees the state as the enemy and vowed to dismantle what he calls the political caste. His win also marked the beginning of Argentina’s realignment with the West, in particular the US.

Milei triumphed again at the midterm elections in October 2025, with his party securing a majority in the legislature. Prior to those elections, the US announced considerable financial assistance to shore up Argentina’s economy (an up to $40 billion bailout package), and President Trump made clear he would consider rescinding it in the event of an opposition victory. While Milei succeeded in bringing inflation under control, the social cost of deep cuts to public spending has dented his initial popularity. His image as an anti-corruption candidate has also taken severe blows, as Milei himself and members of his inner circle have been accused of illicit enrichment. His spokesperson and head of cabinet, Manuel Adorni, was compelled to resign at the end of June 2026 after failing to account for the origin of his sudden and substantial wealth.

In December 2023, Milei rejected the invitation, received during his predecessor’s mandate, to join the enlarged BRICS+ bloc. While during his campaign he vowed to sever all ties with Communist China, this has not materialised in practice given Argentina’s reliance on China as a major trading partner and source of financial support, including a significant currency swap line with the People’s Bank of China. Milei’s administration has also not formally withdrawn from China’s Belt and Road Initiative, which Argentina joined in 2022 with pledges of over $23 billion in Chinese financing and investment. However, criticism of China and its Communist leadership continues among members of the administration. On 30 August 2026, shortly after the devastating floods and landslides on the Nepal-China border, the National Director of Digital Communication, Juan Pablo Carreira Carreira (2026) , published a post on X blaming the management of the Communist Party for the disaster, provoking a formal rebuke from the Chinese embassy (2026) in Buenos Aires.

The next general elections are scheduled for October 2027. Most polls still show Milei ahead, but his political popularity has reached its lowest point since taking office. This context may help explain why the long-standing dispute over the islands known to Britain as the Falklands and to Argentina as the Malvinas has resurfaced on the political agenda. Similar to 1982, when the military junta, facing growing domestic opposition amid mass disappearances and widespread human rights abuses, decided to seize the islands from the United Kingdom, triggering a brief but bloody conflict, it is tempting to see the Malvinas question resurfacing on the political agenda as serving similar domestic political purposes (Tait 2026). Adding a new dimension to the dispute, Trump has signalled that he may reconsider the traditional US position supporting British sovereignty, reportedly as leverage against NATO allies that declined to join the Iran war, while simultaneously seeking to reward partners in the Western Hemisphere.

Bolivia

After nearly two decades of political dominance by the Movement Toward Socialism (Movimiento al Socialismo, MAS), interrupted only briefly in 2019–2020, and three presidential terms under Evo Morales, Bolivia’s first indigenous head of state, the 2025 general elections marked a significant political shift with the election of Rodrigo Paz, son of former president Jaime Paz Zamora. Paz is a social conservative who advocates free market reforms.

Bolivia is an ethnically diverse country in which roughly half the population is of indigenous origin, a group that had long formed the backbone of the MAS movement. However, internal divisions within the party led to a fragmentation of the MAS vote in the 2025 elections, as several younger candidates competed for that same electorate. Paz managed to attract a share of the indigenous vote through his running mate, Edmand Lara, a former police officer who had gained public prominence through his exposure of corruption on social media. This alliance proved decisive in the second round, as no other candidate had indigenous roots. It did not last long, however. Shortly after the election, Lara declared himself in opposition to the government.

Morales, who retains strong support among part of the population, presided over a period of economic growth and poverty reduction largely financed by high commodity prices, particularly hydrocarbons. This economic boom came to an abrupt end in 2023, when Bolivia entered a severe economic crisis characterised by a foreign exchange shortage, a sharp decline in international reserves, a structural fiscal deficit, and growing pressure on the national currency, the boliviano. Against this backdrop, the ruling MAS party became engulfed in an open power struggle between then-president Luis Arce and former president Morales, who was subsequently expelled from the party.

Despite a constitutional term limit on standing for re-election, Morales sought to advance his candidacy for the 2025 race, though his bid was ultimately rejected. He initially called for disruption of the electoral process, triggering sporadic violence across the country, before shifting to promoting a protest vote during the first-round campaign. He remains in hiding in the Chapare coca-growing region, where he commands strong social support, as multiple arrest warrants place him at risk of detention. He is facing a human-trafficking case related to allegations that he fathered a child with a fifteen-year-old girl during his presidency; having failed to appear at trial, a further arrest warrant was issued. The case dates to 2020, during the brief interim presidency of opposition leader Jeanine Áñez. Morales’ legal team has characterised it as political persecution (Ruvenal 2026).

When Paz moved to slash long-standing fuel subsidies in order to reduce the budget deficit, widespread protests erupted, culminating in fifty days of highway blockades by labour unions and indigenous groups that brought the country to a standstill and pushed the government to the brink of collapse. The blockades had a cascading effect on the economy and society, with soaring prices putting basic goods out of reach for many Bolivians and hospitals facing shortages of medicines. The situation was brought to an end after Paz declared a state of emergency and authorised greater military involvement in restoring road access. Morales backed the protests, leading to a new arrest warrant issued in July 2026 for alleged armed uprising against the state. In light of the rising social discontent, Morales told journalists that he is contemplating a return to politics (Garrison 2026).

The arrest of Fernando Cerimedo on 18 August in Bolivia, on charges of allegedly plotting to kill his girlfriend, Bolivian lawyer Nadia Beller, had far-reaching political consequences, given that he had served as a presidential adviser to Paz (Rogero 2026). An Argentine political consultant known for his use of bot farms, Cerimedo had been involved with several of Latin America’s new wave of far-right leaders (Milei in Argentina, Kast in Chile, Bolsonaro in Brazil, and Asfura in Honduras). His former girlfriend began publicly releasing photographs and private communications, exposing the extent of his role and revealing that he had played a significantly larger part in Paz’s government than had been publicly acknowledged. Vice-president Lara added further pressure by asserting that Cerimedo had exercised real power and taken decisions within the Paz administration, and published unverified audio recordings attributed to conversations involving Cerimedo and members of Paz’s inner circle, including his wife. Beller also claimed to hold evidence of alleged irregular business dealings involving Paz and his family (Lissardy 2026).

In July 2026, Bolivia reached a $1.9 billion staff-level agreement with the IMF, although the agreement still requires approval from the IMF’s Executive Board and Bolivia’s Congress. If approved, the deal would mark Bolivia’s first multi-year IMF arrangement since 2006 and the reforms associated with the programme are likely to have significant structural consequences and could generate further social unrest..

On the foreign policy front, Paz has overseen a significant reorientation of Bolivia towards Washington after nearly two decades of strained relations under Morales, who famously expelled the US ambassador, the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA), and USAID. Paz lifted visa requirements for US citizens, welcomed back a US ambassador, secured multilateral financing including from the Washington-based Inter-American Development Bank, and invited the DEA to resume operations in the country. He has also courted US technology giants such as Amazon, Tesla, and Oracle to invest in Bolivia’s lithium and rare earth mineral industries (Swift and Bearak 2026).

Brazil

Brazil enters the 2026 presidential election deeply polarised between left-wing incumbent Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and far-right senator Flávio Bolsonaro, son of former president Jair Bolsonaro. The election continues the political conflict that has dominated Brazil since Bolsonaro’s rise in 2018. Bolsonaro’s refusal to accept his defeat by Lula in 2022 culminated in the 8 January 2023 attacks in Brasília, when thousands of his supporters stormed and ransacked the presidential palace, Congress and Supreme Court in an attempt to create conditions for overturning Lula’s victory. In September 2025, Bolsonaro was convicted by the Supreme Court of organising a coup and attempting to violently abolish democracy, among other crimes, and sentenced to 27 years and three months in prison. Despite being barred from running, he remains highly influential, and Flávio has emerged as the leading candidate of the Bolsonaro movement. With polls showing Lula and Flávio effectively tied in a potential second round, the election is likely to be one of the closest in recent Brazilian history.

Lula’s government has combined social redistribution with an attempt to revive Brazilian industry and reduce the country’s dependence on commodity exports. The economic environment, however, has become less favourable. GDP grew by 0.5% in the second quarter of 2026, down from 1.1% in the first quarter, while inflation stood at 4.24%, above the central bank’s 3% target. The benchmark interest rate remained at 14%, contributing to weaker household consumption and tighter financial conditions (Araujo 2026).

The economic and foreign-policy dimensions are closely connected, particularly through Brazil’s relationship with China. Lula wants to reindustrialise Brazil by developing sectors such as electric vehicles, renewable energy and critical minerals, but Chinese companies are increasingly competing in these same industries. At the same time, China is indispensable to Brazil as a market for its agricultural and mineral exports. Brazil therefore needs Chinese trade and investment while also seeking to protect domestic producers from Chinese competition. This creates a structural dilemma that is likely to constrain any government, regardless of its ideological orientation (Bolle 2026). The dilemma is particularly pronounced on the Brazilian right. Jair Bolsonaro combined close political alignment with Washington with rhetoric hostile to Beijing, even though his agricultural base depended heavily on Chinese demand for soy, beef and other commodities. Flávio Bolsonaro faces a similar contradiction: he is closely aligned with Donald Trump and would likely seek stronger economic and political ties with Washington, but a substantial reduction in Brazil’s dependence on China would conflict with the economic interests of important parts of his electorate (Bolle 2026).

This tension has become increasingly visible in Brazil’s relations with the United States. The Trump administration has imposed a 25% tariff on Brazilian imports, designated two of Brazil’s largest criminal organisations as foreign terrorist organisations, and adopted increasingly confrontational rhetoric towards the Lula government. Brazilian officials suspect this is partly designed to boost Bolsonaro by making Lula look soft on crime. In July, two Trump officials had been denied entry visas because they planned “to meddle in Brazil’s ​elections” according to Lula (Phillips 2026).

Brazilian concerns about interference have also extended to diplomatic appointments. The US ambassadorial post in Brasília had been vacant since January 2025 and Trump nominated Florida politician Daniel Perez, a close ally of Secretary of State Marco Rubio, on 1 June 2026, shortly before the elections. Brazil has withheld the diplomatic agrément required for him to take up the post arguing that if there was no urgency before it can now also wait until after the elections. Brazilian officials regarded the nomination as particularly sensitive because Washington announced it without first obtaining Brazil’s consent, contrary to normal diplomatic practice. The dispute escalated when the United States subsequently revoked the visa of Brazil’s ambassador in Washington. Brazilian authorities have presented the episode, together with US pressure over tariffs and the attempted visit of US officials whom Brasília suspected of seeking to influence the election, as part of a broader concern about American interference in Brazil’s electoral process (Phillips 2026).

The dispute is particularly significant because Washington’s preferred geopolitical orientation would differ substantially from Lula’s. Brazil under Lula has strengthened its relationship with China, promoted BRICS and advocated a more multipolar international order. Brazil is also the only Latin American country that is a full member of BRICS. A Bolsonaro-led government would likely move closer to Washington, seek to reduce economic dependence on China and provide greater access for US companies to sectors such as technology and critical minerals. Such a shift would have important implications for Brazil’s industrial policy, given that Chinese firms are already deeply involved in many of these strategic sectors (Bolle 2026).

The 2026 election is therefore about more than the continuation of Brazil’s Lula–Bolsonaro polarisation. It also raises a broader question about the country’s international and economic orientation. Lula seeks to preserve strategic autonomy through closer relations with China and BRICS while rebuilding domestic industry; Flávio Bolsonaro represents a potentially much closer alignment with Washington. Yet neither side can easily escape Brazil’s structural dependence on both powers.

Colombia

The 2026 presidential elections saw the sudden rise and ultimate victory of Abelardo De La Espriella. Backed by Trump (Correal 2026), the wealthy criminal defence lawyer with no previous political experience represents another win for the far right in Latin America.

Colombia was long dominated by centrist and centre-right parties, with the notable exception of Gustavo Petro’s presidency (2022-2026), the country’s first left-wing head of state. However, De La Espriella’s victory is not simply a return to conservative rule. He portrayed himself as an anti-establishment outsider, an efficient cost-cutter in the spirit of Milei and a hardliner on security in the sense of El Salvador’s Nayib Bukele. While projecting an outsider image, he nevertheless chose a respected former commerce minister, José Manuel Restrepo, as his running mate, most likely to broaden his electoral appeal. Once in office, the budget he presented exceeded by 10 per cent that of Petro (Freixes 2026), whom he had criticised throughout the campaign for overspending.

De La Espriella holds dual Colombian and US citizenship and has close ties to the United States, having lived in Florida for more than a decade and been naturalised as a US citizen in 2023. In Miami, he built his reputation representing high-profile Colombian clients, many of them charged with drug trafficking and corruption or linked to right-wing paramilitaries, precisely the kind of clientele he now vows to pursue as president. Shortly after taking his presidential oath of office on 7 August 2026 in Cali, he travelled to the Pichincha Battalion, two kilometres from the inauguration venue, to deliver his inaugural address directly to the commanders of the military and police forces, outlining the main priorities of his administration. He announced that one of his first acts in government would be to sign the Shield of the Americas, the initiative launched by President Trump on 7 March 2026 aimed at countering cartel operations across the region, signalling his intention to take a leading role in the fight against organised crime. Following the example of Bukele in El Salvador, he announced the construction of mega-prisons to house those deemed the greatest threat to public safety. He also announced the resumption of aerial fumigation using herbicides to combat coca plantations, the authorisation of fracking for oil and gas extraction, and the abolition of the wealth tax (Epoch Times Español 2026).

Previous president Petro had pledged sweeping social transformation, yet his reform agenda was repeatedly slowed or blocked by a fragmented Congress, judicial scrutiny, and institutional resistance. His administration presided over moderate economic growth but a meaningful reduction in monetary poverty, which fell from 36.6 per cent in 2022 to 28.0 per cent in 2025 (DANE 2026). His flagship Paz Total policy fell short of its stated objectives, as negotiations with armed groups produced limited progress while illegal armed actors expanded their territorial control and recruitment. This deteriorating security environment contributed to US sanctions in 2025, when Petro, his wife, his son, and a close associate were placed on the Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) sanctions list for allegedly permitting drug cartels to operate with impunity (U.S. Department of the Treasury 2025). De La Espriella, by contrast, was rewarded on the day of his inauguration with the announcement of a $1 billion aid package for Colombia from the United States (U.S. Department of State, Office of the Spokesperson 2026).

The foreign-policy shift has extended beyond Colombia’s closer alignment with Washington to a striking realignment with Israel. Petro had broken diplomatic relations with Israel and adopted a strongly pro-Palestinian position over the Gaza war. De La Espriella has moved in the opposite direction, seeking to restore close political, security and intelligence ties with Israel, announcing plans to move Colombia’s embassy to Jerusalem and cancelling Petro’s plans to open an embassy in Ramallah. His government went further on 10 August, making Colombia only the second country in the world, after the United States, to officially recognise Israeli sovereignty over the occupied Golan Heights, breaking with the prevailing international position that the territory remains Syrian.

- The alignment was also visible in the government’s response to the devastating earthquake that struck Colombia three days after De La Espriella took office; although several countries offered assistance, the foreign search-and-rescue teams initially admitted by Bogotá came from the United States, Israel, Ecuador and El Salvador, while teams offered by countries including Mexico and Brazil were not initially accepted.

Haiti

Haitian officials have set elections for Dec. 13, but many experts say extreme violence is likely to force a postponement.

Elections — the first in a decade — are scheduled for Dec. 13, but kidnappings and killings are pervasive.

Mr. Bien-Aimé, a candidate for the Haitian Senate, is one of a growing chorus of voices who believe the Haitian government, under pressure from the United States, has rushed to hold elections at any cost. Critics argue that too many swaths of the Haitian electorate have been forced from their homes and would be unable to cast ballots.

Many people believe the election date is essentially fiction — a goal that even Haitian officials do not believe possible.

But as Haiti enters its second decade without elections to fill a single government office, the push for a return to democracy is real.

While many people argue that you can’t hold elections without security, government officials and some experts strongly argue the opposite: that Haiti will never be safe if it doesn’t start electing its own leaders.

Haiti last held elections in November 2016, when Jovenel Moïse, a banana exporter, was elected amid delays and accusations of widespread fraud. He was assassinated in July 2021, and then the gang violence that had always been common in Haiti quickly spread. In early 2024, it exploded into a kind of war.

Armed groups that had fought each other for years, sometimes doing the bidding of corrupt politicians, joined forces. They attacked police stations, prisons, hospitals and neighborhoods. The prime minister was forced out and the airport was closed for months.

Gangs control some 70 percent of Haiti’s capital, Port-au-Prince, and have spread to other regions outside the city. With the security situation so volatile, elections were postponed several times. The race to elect the legislature is now six years overdue.

The United States has spent hundreds of millions of dollars on security efforts in Haiti, and experts say Washington has been a big factor in pressing Haitian officials to move forward.

Honduras

Honduras was governed by the conservative Partido Nacional for twelve consecutive years, from 2010 to 2022, first under Porfirio Lobo and then under Juan Orlando Hernández (JOH), with the sole interruption being the presidency of Xiomara Castro (2022–2026), wife of Manuel Zelaya, who was ousted in a coup in 2009. The victory of Nasry “Tito” Asfura in the November 2025 elections therefore represented a return to power for the party that had dominated Honduran politics for much of the preceding decade. His victory was exceptionally narrow, with a margin of roughly 27,000 votes, and remains contested as the runner-up, Salvador Nasralla, refuses to recognise the result and alleges electoral fraud. One development that has intensified the allegations is the publication in August 2026 of audio recordings by a former member of the electoral tribunal, Marlon Ochoa, in which Argentine political consultant Fernando Cerimedo, who advised the Partido Nacional campaign, is heard stating that votes were introduced in favour of Asfura in certain ballot boxes and that votes unfavourable to the candidate were removed (Duarte Galleguillos 2026).

Asfura’s victory was also closely associated with Donald Trump, who openly intervened in the campaign days before the election, endorsing Asfura and warning that US support could be reconsidered if he lost (Wagner and Ernst 2025). Two days before the election, Trump also announced a full pardon for Juan Orlando Hernández (Correal et al. 2025), the former president convicted in 2024 by a US federal court of conspiring to import cocaine into the United States and sentenced to 45 years in prison. On paper, JOH had been a close US ally in the fight against drugs, as his governments cooperated extensively with Washington on counternarcotics and security. Yet US prosecutors subsequently alleged that he had used the Honduran state and his political power to facilitate the trafficking of more than 400 tonnes of cocaine to the United States in exchange for millions of dollars in bribes. His younger brother, Juan Antonio “Tony” Hernández, a former Honduran congressman, was arrested in 2018, convicted in the United States of cocaine trafficking, and sentenced to life imprisonment plus 30 years.

The pardon acquired a new dimension in 2026 following the publication of a series of leaked recordings known as “Hondurasgate”. The recordings, made between January and April 2026 and published in April, purport to document conversations involving Hernández, Asfura and Vice President María Antonieta Mejía. In the recordings, Hernández claims that individuals linked to Israel, including Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, were involved in securing and financing his pardon (Hondurasgate 2026a). The recordings further allege that Hernández, with support from Republican allies of Trump and Argentine President Javier Milei, discussed establishing a conservative media operation capable of attacking the governments of Gustavo Petro in Colombia and Claudia Sheinbaum in Mexico, while planning his own return to the Honduran presidency in exchange for granting the United States a new military base and expanded control over Honduras’s special economic zones (Hondurasgate 2026b).

The case therefore illustrates a striking paradox. Hernández was convicted by the US justice system after prosecutors accused him of facilitating hundreds of tonnes of cocaine shipments, yet Trump subsequently pardoned him and backed the candidate of Hernández’s own party in a closely contested election. The pardon allowed Hernández to return to Honduras and resume his political influence in the country.
