Argentina

In the 2023 elections, Javier Milei won a resounding victory against Economy Minister Sergio Massa. During the previous Peronist government, annual inflation had reached 138 per cent, and Milei’s promise to bring it under control found fertile ground among voters desperate for change. His win marked the beginning of a dramatic ideological shift in Argentina and the wider region; which had been dominated by left-wing governments for much of the preceding decade. Milei describes himself as an “anarcho-capitalist” who sees the state as the enemy and vowed to dismantle what he calls the political caste. His win also marked the beginning of Argentina’s realignment with the West, in particular the US.

Milei triumphed again at the midterm elections in October 2025, with his party securing a majority in the legislature. Prior to those elections, the US announced considerable financial assistance to shore up Argentina’s economy (an up to $40 billion bailout package), and President Trump made clear he would consider rescinding it in the event of an opposition victory. While Milei succeeded in bringing inflation under control, the social cost of deep cuts to public spending has dented his initial popularity. His image as an anti-corruption candidate has also taken severe blows, as Milei himself and members of his inner circle have been accused of illicit enrichment. His spokesperson and head of cabinet, Manuel Adorni, was compelled to resign at the end of June 2026 after failing to account for the origin of his sudden and substantial wealth.

In December 2023, Milei rejected the invitation, received during his predecessor’s mandate, to join the enlarged BRICS+ bloc. While during his campaign he vowed to sever all ties with Communist China, this has not materialised in practice given Argentina’s reliance on China as a major trading partner and source of financial support, including a significant currency swap line with the People’s Bank of China. Milei’s administration has also not formally withdrawn from China’s Belt and Road Initiative, which Argentina joined in 2022 with pledges of over $23 billion in Chinese financing and investment. However, criticism of China and its Communist leadership continues among members of the administration. On 30 August 2026, shortly after the devastating floods and landslides on the Nepal-China border, the National Director of Digital Communication, Juan Pablo Carreira Carreira (2026) , published a post on X blaming the management of the Communist Party for the disaster, provoking a formal rebuke from the Chinese embassy (2026) in Buenos Aires.

The next general elections are scheduled for October 2027. Most polls still show Milei ahead, but his political popularity has reached its lowest point since taking office. This context may help explain why the long-standing dispute over the islands known to Britain as the Falklands and to Argentina as the Malvinas has resurfaced on the political agenda. Similar to 1982, when the military junta, facing growing domestic opposition amid mass disappearances and widespread human rights abuses, decided to seize the islands from the United Kingdom, triggering a brief but bloody conflict, it is tempting to see the Malvinas question resurfacing on the political agenda as serving similar domestic political purposes (Tait 2026). Adding a new dimension to the dispute, Trump has signalled that he may reconsider the traditional US position supporting British sovereignty, reportedly as leverage against NATO allies that declined to join the Iran war, while simultaneously seeking to reward partners in the Western Hemisphere.

Bolivia

After nearly two decades under the Movement Toward Socialism (Movimiento al Socialismo, MAS), with the brief exception of 2019-2020, and three terms under Evo Morales, Bolivia’s first indigenous head of state, the 2025 general elections marked a significant political shift with the election of Rodrigo Paz, son of former president Jaime Paz Zamora. Paz is a social conservative who advocates free market reforms.

Bolivia is an ethnically diverse country in which roughly half the population is of indigenous origin, a group that had long formed the backbone of the MAS movement. However, internal divisions within the party led to a fragmentation of the MAS vote in the 2025 elections, as several younger candidates competed for that same electorate. Paz managed to attract a share of the indigenous vote through his running mate, Edmand Lara, a former police officer who had gained public prominence through his exposure of corruption on social media. This alliance proved decisive in the second round, as no other candidate had indigenous roots. It did not last long, however. Shortly after the election, Lara declared himself in opposition to the government.

Morales, who retains strong support among part of the population, presided over a period of economic growth and poverty reduction largely financed by high commodity prices, particularly hydrocarbons. This economic boom came to an abrupt end in 2023, when Bolivia entered a severe economic crisis characterised by a foreign exchange shortage, a sharp decline in international reserves, a structural fiscal deficit, and growing pressure on the national currency, the boliviano. Against this backdrop, the ruling MAS party became engulfed in an open power struggle between then-president Luis Arce and former president Morales, who was subsequently expelled from the party.

Despite a constitutional ban on standing for election, Morales sought to advance his candidacy for the 2025 race, though his bid was ultimately rejected. He initially called for disruption of the electoral process, triggering sporadic violence across the country, before shifting to promoting a protest vote during the first-round campaign. He remains in hiding in the Chapare coca-growing region, where he commands strong social support, as multiple arrest warrants place him at risk of detention. He is being tried for human trafficking, specifically for allegedly impregnating a fifteen-year-old girl during his presidency; having failed to appear at trial, a further arrest warrant was issued. The case dates to 2020, during the brief interim presidency of opposition leader Jeanine Áñez. Morales’ legal team has characterised it as political persecution (Ruvenal 2026).

When Paz moved to slash long-standing fuel subsidies in order to reduce the budget deficit, widespread protests erupted, culminating in fifty days of highway blockades by labour unions and indigenous groups that brought the country to a standstill and pushed the government to the brink of collapse. The blockades had a cascading effect on the economy and society, with soaring prices putting basic goods out of reach for many Bolivians and hospitals facing shortages of medicines. The situation only ended after Paz declared a state of emergency and authorised greater military involvement in restoring road access. Morales backed the protests, leading to a new arrest warrant issued in July 2026 for alleged armed uprising against the state. Morales told journalists that he is contemplating a return to politics (Garrison 2026).

The arrest of Fernando Cerimedo on 18 August in Bolivia, on charges of allegedly plotting to kill his girlfriend, Bolivian lawyer Nadia Beller, had far-reaching political consequences, given that he had served as a presidential adviser to Paz (Rogero 2026). An Argentine political consultant known for his use of bot farms, Cerimedo had been involved with several of Latin America’s new wave of far-right leaders (Milei in Argentina, Kast in Chile, Bolsonaro in Brazil, and Asfura in Honduras). His former girlfriend began publicly releasing photographs and private communications, exposing the extent of his role and revealing that he had played a significantly larger part in Paz’s government than had been publicly acknowledged. Vice-president Lara added further pressure by asserting that Cerimedo had exercised real power and taken decisions within the Paz administration, and published unverified audio recordings attributed to conversations involving Cerimedo and members of Paz’s inner circle, including his wife. Paz dismissed Lara’s actions and suggested that what truly concerned him was Lara’s intentions toward the country, a statement that underscored the deepening rift between the two since the start of the administration. Beller also claimed to hold evidence of alleged irregular business dealings involving Paz and his family (Lissardy 2026).

In July 2026, Bolivia reached a $1.9 billion staff-level agreement with the IMF, although the agreement still requires approval from the IMF’s Executive Board and Bolivia’s Congress. If approved, the deal would mark Bolivia’s first multi-year IMF arrangement since 2006 and the ensuing economic reforms are likely to have great structural consequences with possible social upheaval.

On the foreign policy front, Paz has overseen a significant reorientation of Bolivia towards Washington after nearly two decades of hostility under Morales, who famously expelled the US ambassador, the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA), and USAID. Paz lifted visa requirements for US citizens, welcomed back a US ambassador, secured multilateral financing including from the Washington-based Inter-American Development Bank, and invited the DEA to resume operations in the country. He has also courted US technology giants such as Amazon, Tesla, and Oracle to invest in Bolivia’s lithium and rare earth mineral industries (Swift and Bearak 2026).

Brazil

October’s vote will pit the leftwing incumbent, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, against the far-right senator Flávio Bolsonaro, son of the former president Jair Bolsonaro, a Donald Trump ally who is serving a 27-year sentence for plotting a military coup.

Polls suggest a tight race and Trump and his secretary of state, Marco Rubio, have been accused of trying to tip the scales in Bolsonaro’s favour by launching an overt campaign of economic and diplomatic pressure against Lula.

The US measures include imposing 25% tariffs on most Brazilian imports and designating Brazil’s biggest crime groups – Rio’s Red Command and São Paulo’s First Command of the Capital – as foreign terrorist organisations. Brazilian officials suspect this is partly designed to boost Bolsonaro by making Lula look soft on crime.

In July, Lula said two Trump officials had been denied entry visas because they planned “to meddle in Brazil’s ​elections”. Brazilian officials believe the pair, who reportedly planned to meet Flávio Bolsonaro, intended to sow doubt about the reliability of Brazil’s internationally respected voting system – something the Bolsonaros have sought to do repeatedly. Soon after, the visa of Brazil’s ambassador to Washington was revoked.

But Barros sees one key difference between the two US gambits. “[The 1962 campaign] was done covertly, precisely because the CIA knew there would be real public anger in Brazil if people knew the US was bankrolling candidates. So they did it secretly,” he said. “Today, this is being done openly – and on an incomparably larger scale.”

Barros is convinced the US aims to help elect Flávio Bolsonaro, “because Flávio will give them everything they want”, he said. “Flávio will make a rare earths deal no other president would. The Trump administration deposed and captured Ms. Rodríguez’s predecessor, Nicolás Maduro, in January, transforming a country that had been an adversary into something resembling a client state. President Trump said on Sunday that the highly unusual agreement he reached to control a huge portion of Venezuela’s oil reserves would allow the United States to refill the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, which he has been drawing down amid the war with Iran. But that’s unlikely to happen in the short term: Many of the details of the agreement are unclear and new oil projects can take years to get off the ground.

In a social media post on Sunday, President Trump said he would use the oil from his deal in Venezuela to refill the United States’ stockpile of oil, but that’s something of a pipe dream — at least in the short term.

The agreement, announced last week, covers some 65 billion barrels of reserves in the oil-rich country, and amounts to a highly unusual foray involving a partnership with a private company. But many of its details remain unknown, and that’s just one complication for a process that Mr. Trump said would “begin very shortly.”

Many Venezuelans suspected that there was going to be a price to pay for President Trump’s brazen capture of their autocratic leader, Nicolás Maduro, who was arrested by U.S. forces in January.

The Trump administration has cast its deal with Mr. Betancourt as extending U.S. influence in a country where Chinese and Russian companies once exerted considerable control.

However, tracing the history of Venezuela’s oil fields and the firms involved in them over time can be challenging. The country has not made up-to-date records public, and Venezuela recently redefined some production areas, analysts say.

A document reviewed by The Times showed that China Concord Petroleum was participating in at least two of the 17 areas in 2024. A company by that name was placed under U.S. sanctions in 2019 for alleged involvement in transporting Iranian oil. Prosecutors in Spain targeted the Venezuelan oilman Alejandro Betancourt in one money laundering inquiry. Swiss authorities investigated him in another. Both countries requested his extradition from Britain, where Mr. Betancourt lives in a 14-bedroom country estate, and British authorities arrested him less than a year ago, barring him from traveling abroad.

All of it hardly mattered.

As part of the Trump administration’s takeover of Venezuela’s oil industry, the United States is forming an extraordinary partnership with Mr. Betancourt. Under the arrangement, the Pentagon could seek to take a large stake in his company, a deal that defies easy comparison in recent American history, oil analysts say.

That would make Mr. Betancourt the main partner of the Trump administration as it pushes not just to control the sale of Venezuela’s oil, but to own a chunk of it directly. And it would place Mr. Betancourt at the core of Venezuela’s deepening transformation from a sovereign nation into essentially a vassal state of the United States.

Under the deal with the Trump administration, a financing office within the Pentagon plans to partner with Mr. Betancourt’s oil company, North American Blue Energy Partners, or NABEP, which is being granted 100-year concessions to operate 17 oil fields in Venezuela.

Mr. Betancourt does not own the fields, but the concessions give him the exclusive rights to exploit them — and they are potentially enormous. In all, these oil fields have estimated reserves of 65 billion barrels, nearly as much as all the proven reserves in the United States.
