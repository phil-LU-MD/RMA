Rethinking hedging: towards a definition appropriate for Latin America

Author

Philippe Decker

Published

1/11/26

I would make the central argument of the article that the concept of hedging, as developed in the literature on Southeast Asia, cannot be transferred directly to Latin America because the underlying geopolitical and security conditions are substantially different. What does the current literature say? the need of a security risk… In particular, China does not present the region with the kind of direct military challenge that underpins much of the original literature focusing on Southeast Asia and the South China Sea. Conversely, the United States, traditionally the region’s dominant security power, can itself represent a source of risk to the sovereignty and strategic autonomy of Latin American countries.

There are already several studies applying hedging to Latin America, including Cook et al. (2024), Sims and Lee (2025), and a particularly recent article by Adins (2026) focusing on Brazil. The contribution here could therefore be to reconsider the underlying conceptual assumptions rather than simply demonstrate that Latin American countries hedge.

The conceptual problem: can hedging be transferred to Latin America? The concept of hedging has become increasingly popular in international relations, particularly in explaining how small and middle powers respond to the growing competition between the United States and China. Yet, as Haacke (2019) demonstrates, the concept remains loosely defined. Different authors understand hedging as a risk-management strategy, an alignment choice or a combination of apparently contradictory foreign policy practices (be more precise who defends which position here). This conceptual ambiguity has also produced considerable disagreement (not disagreement but misaligned classification) about which countries can actually be considered to be hedging. Haacke’s principal contribution is to return to the original connection between hedging and risk, arguing that it should be understood as a response to a perceived security risk rather than an acknowledged security threat. This distinction is important because balancing and bandwagoning are conventionally understood as responses to threats, whereas hedging is intended to manage uncertainty and reduce exposure to risks that may materialise in the future (Haacke 2019).

The applicability of this distinction to Latin America is not straightforward. Much of the original literature emerged from the strategic circumstances of Southeast Asia, where China’s growing economic and military power has created both economic opportunities and security concerns. Countries such as Vietnam, the Philippines and Malaysia must accommodate China’s economic importance while managing its territorial claims and expanding military presence. In this context, maintaining economic engagement with Beijing while preserving US military involvement can constitute a strategy for mitigating the security risks associated with China’s rise (Goh 2005b, 2006).

The situation in Latin America is fundamentally different. Although China has become a major, and in several countries the principal, economic partner, its presence is predominantly commercial, financial and diplomatic. Chinese military engagement is not entirely absent: it includes arms sales (where, what and what amount?), military exchanges, training and institutional cooperation (specify also for military exchanges, training and cooperation. which countries?). However, China has not established permanent military bases in the region or developed a comparable military presence to that of the United States. Nor do its territorial claims or military activities present Latin American governments with the kind of direct security challenge experienced by several Southeast Asian countries (Ellis 2020).

Consequently, the assumption that Latin American countries engage economically with China while maintaining military cooperation with the United States to deter a potential Chinese threat is difficult to sustain. Their alignment with Washington is more often associated with ideological affinity, domestic political preferences, security dependence or the economic and political benefits of closer relations with the United States. Participation in US-led initiatives, such as Trump’s Shield of the Americas, or the provision of military access and facilities cannot automatically be interpreted as balancing against China. These relationships may instead reflect a decision to align politically and strategically with Washington, irrespective of whether Beijing is perceived as a security threat.

The asymmetry of the regional relationship is therefore important. While the United States retains overwhelming military predominance and extensive security relationships across the hemisphere, China offers an alternative primarily through trade, investment, infrastructure and diplomatic cooperation. This does not mean that Chinese economic dependence is without strategic implications, but it does make the conventional security-based understanding of hedging less immediately applicable.

Several of the principal definitions in the literature illustrate these difficulties. Ciorciari’s (2007, 2010) concept of limited alignment provides one example. He understands limited alignment as a way for developing countries to obtain the benefits of security cooperation with a great power while avoiding the constraints of excessive dependence. Such alignment excludes commitments requiring support for a great power’s military operations, adherence to particular foreign policy positions or the provision of permanent military bases (Ciorciari 2007, 2010). Applied to Latin America, this definition would potentially exclude countries hosting US military facilities or accepting substantial security commitments that constrain their foreign policy autonomy. It also raises the question of whether participation in initiatives such as the Shield of the Americas constitutes a form of alignment that goes beyond limited alignment. More generally, the distinction between hedging and formal alignment remains problematic when states maintain extensive military and security relationships with a major power. As Haacke (2019) points out, the literature itself remains divided on whether a country formally allied to a great power can nevertheless be considered to be hedging.

Evelyn Goh’s understanding of hedging as a combination of engagement and indirect balancing is potentially more applicable. She argues that the distinction between hedging and balancing depends on whether balancing measures are accompanied by policies promoting engagement and reassurance. In her account, Southeast Asian hedging combines strong engagement with China and the facilitation of a continuing US strategic presence as a counterweight to Chinese power (Goh 2005a, 2006). However, the second component of this approach is difficult to transpose to Latin America. There is no equivalent Chinese military threat that would explain the need to facilitate a continuing US strategic presence as a deterrent. Moreover, simultaneous economic engagement with China and security cooperation with the United States is not necessarily evidence of hedging if the latter is not intended to mitigate a perceived security risk.

Kuik’s conceptualisation of hedging as a combination of opposing behaviours, ranging from power acceptance to power rejection, offers greater flexibility. It includes economic engagement, diplomatic accommodation, resistance and diversification within a broader strategy of managing great-power uncertainty. This approach could readily encompass many Latin American countries, where governments simultaneously pursue Chinese investment and trade while maintaining political, economic and military relations with Washington. Its principal weakness, however, is that it risks making hedging an excessively broad residual category. Almost any country maintaining relationships with competing powers could qualify, even when its policies are driven by economic pragmatism or domestic political considerations rather than a deliberate effort to manage external risks (Kuik 2016, 2021).

Van Jackson’s (2014) contribution raises a further methodological question. He identifies military strengthening without a declared adversary as one of the principal indicators of hedging. This could be relevant to Latin American countries seeking to strengthen their defence capabilities without explicitly identifying the United States or another major power as a potential adversary (Jackson 2014). Nevertheless, such measures are difficult to distinguish from conventional military modernisation, internal security requirements or efforts to protect territorial sovereignty. Limited military cooperation can also be interpreted as soft balancing. As Haacke points out, military strengthening alone is therefore insufficient to establish hedging. Its purpose and the security perceptions underlying it must also be examined (Haacke 2019).

The United States as a source of risk. A more appropriate starting point for the Latin American context may be to reconsider which external power constitutes the principal source of risk. Rather than assuming that the risk originates from China’s emergence as a military power, it may be more relevant to examine the vulnerabilities created by US regional hegemony itself.

The United States has historically exercised disproportionate political, economic and military influence over Latin America. Its ability to impose sanctions, restrict market access, exert diplomatic pressure and, ultimately, resort to military force means that it can represent a source of risk to the sovereignty and strategic autonomy of governments that pursue an independent foreign policy. The January 2026 military operation in Venezuela, which resulted in the capture of Nicolás Maduro, illustrates how military intervention can become a concrete concern rather than merely a historical precedent. It also raises questions about the vulnerability of other governments that may fall outside Washington’s political preferences.

Brazil provides a particularly relevant case. Lula strongly condemned the US operation against Venezuela, describing it as a violation of sovereignty and a dangerous precedent for the region. Following the intervention, Brazilian officials initially assessed that the operation posed little direct military risk to Brazil and did not consider additional troop deployments along the Venezuelan border necessary. The immediate concerns were primarily humanitarian and related to potential refugee movements (Gielow 2026).

Nevertheless, the episode appears to have prompted a reassessment of Brazil’s own defence capabilities. In May 2026, Defence Minister José Múcio acknowledged that Brazil would not be prepared to withstand a comparable US military operation. In August, Lula explicitly called for greater investment in defence, arguing that Brazil needed to be prepared so that no foreign power could invade the country and remove its president as had happened in Venezuela. He emphasised the importance of developing the domestic defence industry and preparing for peace rather than war (Amado Mundo 2026; Reuters 2026).

These statements suggest that the US operation has contributed to a more explicit perception of external security risk among Brazil’s political leadership. However, it is important to distinguish political declarations from concrete changes in military policy. Brazil’s National Defence Policy and National Defence Strategy, approved in November 2025, already emphasised deterrence, sovereignty and the need to strengthen national defence capabilities. They therefore cannot be interpreted as a direct response to the January 2026 intervention (Presidency of the Republic of Brazil 2025). The evidence so far points more clearly to a change in political discourse and the prioritisation of defence than to a completed military reorientation specifically directed against the United States.

This raises the question of whether Brazil constitutes a contemporary example of hedging against US power. Its extensive economic relationship with China, participation in BRICS, pursuit of strategic autonomy and efforts to develop domestic defence capabilities could be interpreted as ways of reducing dependence on Washington. Yet it remains unclear whether these policies constitute a deliberate strategy to mitigate a perceived US security risk or reflect a longer-standing foreign policy tradition of diversification and autonomy. Brazil is certainly an important candidate for examining this question, but it would be premature to consider it the only Latin American country pursuing hedging. Existing research has also identified Panama and Uruguay as potential hedgers under broader definitions, while other countries may exhibit elements of risk-management behaviour (Sims and Lee 2025).

Towards a definition appropriate for Latin America. The preceding discussion suggests that hedging should not be defined exclusively as a strategy through which countries manage the security risks posed by a rising China while maintaining US military protection. Such a definition presupposes a regional security environment that does not adequately reflect Latin American realities. (Does it make sense to use the term, hedging, in the Latin American context?)

Haacke’s emphasis on the original connection between hedging and risk nevertheless offers a useful starting point. The relevant question is not simply whether a country maintains economic and political relations with competing great powers, but whether its leadership deliberately seeks to reduce vulnerabilities arising from uncertainty about the intentions or potential coercive actions of one or more of them.

For Latin America, this could involve countries seeking to reduce their exposure to US political, economic or military pressure by diversifying international partnerships, strengthening autonomous national capabilities and cultivating diplomatic relationships that offer alternative sources of support. Engagement with China, Russia or other actors could form part of such a strategy, but would not necessarily imply that these countries represent equivalent military alternatives to the United States. Conversely, maintaining close relations with Washington while engaging economically with Beijing would not, in itself, constitute hedging.

A preliminary definition could therefore be formulated as follows: Hedging in Latin America is a deliberate risk-management strategy through which governments seek to preserve foreign policy autonomy and reduce their vulnerability to potential coercion or dependence by a major power. It involves maintaining selective engagement with the power representing the perceived risk while diversifying economic, diplomatic and, where appropriate, security relationships with other actors, alongside efforts to strengthen domestic capabilities. The defining characteristic is not the simultaneous pursuit of contradictory foreign policy practices, but the intention to preserve strategic flexibility and avoid excessive dependence in the face of uncertain external pressures.

This definition differs from conventional approaches in three respects. (the idea is not to create one more definition but to adapt the existing theoretical framework to LA). First, the source of risk need not be a rising power such as China; it can also be the established regional hegemon, the United States. Second, the alternative partnerships pursued need not provide an equivalent military counterweight. They may instead offer economic diversification, diplomatic leverage or technological alternatives. Third, the existence of multiple international partnerships is insufficient to establish hedging: there must be evidence that these relationships are deliberately pursued to mitigate a perceived risk.

It is also important to distinguish hedging from the broader pursuit of strategic autonomy. The latter represents an objective or general orientation of foreign policy, whereas hedging constitutes a possible strategy through which governments seek to preserve that autonomy under conditions of uncertainty. Similarly, foreign policy diversification is not necessarily hedging. It becomes relevant to the concept only when it serves an identifiable risk-management purpose.

This distinction could provide a way to retain the analytical value of Haacke’s contribution while adapting it to the particularities of the Latin American international environment.

Operationalisation: towards a multilayer approach. Identifying hedging empirically remains difficult. As the preceding literature demonstrates, military cooperation, arms purchases, diplomatic alignment and economic diversification can all be interpreted in different ways depending on the conceptual framework adopted. No single variable is likely to establish hedging conclusively.

This may provide a justification for a multilayer network approach. Rather than attempting to identify hedging through one indicator, the analysis could examine the relationships between Latin American countries and major external powers across several interconnected dimensions: - Economic layer: trade, foreign direct investment, loans, infrastructure projects and dependence on strategic imports and exports. - Military and security layer: arms transfers, military exercises, training, intelligence cooperation, basing arrangements, access agreements and defence partnerships. - Diplomatic and political layer: voting patterns in international organisations, bilateral agreements, public statements, participation in international coalitions and diplomatic support. - Coercion and vulnerability layer: exposure to sanctions, tariffs, diplomatic pressure, restrictions on market access and other instruments of external influence. The network structure would allow the analysis to identify different patterns of dependence, cooperation and alignment, as well as changes in these relationships over time. It could also distinguish between countries maintaining relationships with multiple powers and those whose relationships are concentrated around a single dominant partner.

However, the network analysis cannot by itself establish whether a country is hedging. The central element of Haacke’s framework remains relevant: the intentions and perceptions of political leadership. The network structure would therefore need to be complemented by an analysis of defence strategies, foreign policy statements and the political discourse surrounding major international developments. These sources would help establish whether particular relationships are deliberately intended to mitigate perceived risks or simply reflect economic pragmatism, ideological alignment or historical institutional ties.

In this sense, a multilayer approach could provide a more systematic empirical framework for applying the concept, while avoiding the assumption that any combination of economic engagement and security cooperation constitutes hedging. 6. Preliminary direction for the article The central argument could ultimately be that Latin America requires a different conceptual application of hedging, rather than an entirely new concept. The emphasis should remain on risk management, as proposed by Haacke, but the identification of relevant risks must reflect the asymmetric regional distribution of power.

China’s growing economic presence does not necessarily constitute a military security threat, while US dominance can itself generate risks for governments seeking to preserve their sovereignty and foreign policy autonomy. Brazil offers an important contemporary case for examining whether these risks are generating more deliberate hedging behaviour. Whether other countries qualify will require a systematic comparative assessment rather than an assumption based on their economic or diplomatic relationships.

The resulting contribution would be twofold: first, to clarify the conditions under which hedging can be meaningfully applied to Latin America; and second, to develop a multidimensional empirical approach capable of distinguishing hedging from conventional alignment, balancing, non-alignment and simple foreign policy diversification.
